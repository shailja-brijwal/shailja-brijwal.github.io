Flagship Case Study

Early Engagement Analytics for Student Risk

Turning fragmented digital engagement data into an interpretable, equity-aware early-warning framework for timely student support.

12M+ Daily engagement records
117K+ Student-course pairs
11 Institutional datasets
0.86 Final ROC-AUC

Client

Planning and Information Office (PIO), University of Auckland | Waipapa Taumata Rau

My Role

Project Lead & Modelling Co-Lead

Methods

Python · Feature Engineering · Statistical Testing · Logistic Regression · Random Forest · XGBoost · Power BI

01 · Business Challenge

Identifying risk early enough to make support useful

Academic risk often becomes visible only after missed assessments, declining grades or sustained disengagement — when the opportunity for meaningful intervention is already narrowing.

The challenge was therefore not simply to predict who might fail. The solution needed to identify risk early, produce manageable intervention cohorts, explain why a student-course pair had been flagged, and support equitable human decision-making.

02 · Data & Feature Engineering

Turning fragmented operational data into behavioural signals

Eleven institutional datasets were integrated across different levels of granularity, including daily Canvas engagement, academic outcomes, demographics, course characteristics and assessment structures.

The common analytical unit became the student-course pair, recognising that the same student can engage very differently across different courses.

Raw engagement data were transformed into interpretable behavioural features capturing:

Recency Consistency Engagement decay Assessment responsiveness Engagement intensity Persona behaviour

03 · Behavioural Segmentation

Four engagement personas made early risk interpretable

Early login behaviour was translated into four student-course engagement personas. Statistical testing confirmed that persona membership was meaningfully associated with academic outcomes.

Keen Beans

5.5% failure rate

Early and comparatively engaged.

Browsers

5.2% failure rate

Moderate early engagement.

Hibernators

6.9% failure rate

Persistently weaker engagement.

Late Starters

10.9% failure rate

Highest-risk and largest group, representing 51.8% of persona-labelled student-course pairs.

The personas were designed as behavioural support signals rather than labels of student motivation or ability.

04 · Intervention Timing

Week 4 emerged as the practical decision point

By Week 4, more than 98% of student-course pairs had generated at least one Canvas login and behavioural features such as recency, consistency and engagement intensity had begun to stabilise.

Waiting longer would improve certainty but reduce the remaining time for meaningful support. Week 4 therefore became the operational balance between signal maturity and intervention lead time.

05 · Predictive Modelling

Choosing the model meant balancing detection with operational workload

Because only around 8.5% of student-course outcomes were failures, accuracy alone would have been misleading. The modelling therefore prioritised ROC-AUC, fail recall and fail precision.

Model ROC-AUC Recall Precision
Logistic Regression 0.79 0.66 0.21
Feature-Selected Logistic 0.76 0.61 0.19
Random Forest 0.85 0.23 0.84
XGBoost 0.86 0.67* 0.33*

*At the selected operational threshold of 0.55.

Key Operational Result

Approximately 3,000 fewer false-positive alerts

At the selected 0.55 threshold, XGBoost identified approximately two-thirds of genuine failures while improving precision to roughly one in three flagged records.

Compared with the Logistic Regression baseline, this reduced false-positive alerts by more than 3,000 while retaining useful recall — creating a more credible review list for staff-led triage.

06 · What the Analysis Revealed

Risk was concentrated, not system-wide

The overall pass rate was 91.5%. The real business challenge was finding the smaller group of student-course pairs where support could make the greatest difference.

A single login flag was not enough

Engagement trajectories showed that students could recover or deteriorate over time, supporting the use of recency, consistency and decay features rather than a single engagement snapshot.

Equity gaps extended beyond engagement

Pacific and Māori pass-rate gaps were not explained simply by persona composition, suggesting that behavioural analytics should trigger support rather than be treated as a complete explanation of academic outcomes.

Implementation needed prioritisation

Science was identified as the strongest pilot candidate because it combined the largest student-course volume with the lowest faculty pass rate.

07 · From Model to Action

Power BI turned probability scores into a staff workflow

A risk score alone was not operationally useful. The project therefore translated XGBoost output into a Power BI decision-support layer with three levels of review:

1

Executive Overview

Leadership view of overall risk scale and distribution across faculties and student groups.

2

Risk Deep-Dive

Analyst view explaining why records are being flagged using engagement and course-level context.

3

Student-Course Detail

Action layer where staff can review specific flagged records before deciding whether outreach is appropriate.

Design principle: The dashboard supports staff judgement. It is not an automated decision-making system.

08 · Recommendations

Turning early insight into early action

01

Introduce a staged workflow: simple activation checks in Week 1 followed by model-supported triage from Week 4.

02

Tailor outreach to behavioural personas rather than using generic communication for every flagged student.

03

Embed equity review and culturally appropriate support pathways within the intervention process.

04

Deploy XGBoost risk outputs through Power BI as a human-led decision-support tool.

05

Validate performance on additional semesters and establish governance before full production deployment.

09 · Responsible Implementation

Prediction is a support signal, not a judgement

Canvas engagement is only a proxy for learning behaviour, and the analysis was based on a single semester. Students may learn through channels that are not visible in Canvas, and outcome disparities cannot be assumed to be caused by engagement behaviour.

The recommended implementation therefore preserves staff judgement, requires subgroup monitoring, and calls for further semester validation, privacy controls and clear student communication protocols.

The Takeaway

The value of learning analytics is not predicting failure itself — it is creating enough lead time for meaningful support.
